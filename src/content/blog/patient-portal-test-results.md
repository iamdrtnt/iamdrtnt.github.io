A patient sees a red flag beside a blood test result on a Friday evening. The portal gives a number and a reference interval. It gives no indication of whether the finding explains the symptoms, whether anyone has reviewed it or when the clinic will call. By Monday, the practice has a message from a worried patient, and the result is still in the ordering clinician’s inbox. In this hypothetical example, the portal did exactly what it was configured to do. The gaps are in explanation and follow-up.

Early access can give patients time to prepare questions, check that a promised result arrived and bring their records to another appointment. It also makes the interval between a result becoming available and someone's explaining it more visible. The evidence supports keeping access, giving patients better context and designing a separate workflow for clinical action. These three aims require different measures.

## What patients say about seeing results early

In a 2023 survey at four US academic medical centres, 7,520 of 7,859 respondents who answered the preference question (95.7%) wanted to continue receiving results immediately, even before a clinician contacted them. Among respondents who described receiving a nonnormal result and answered that question, 2,337 of 2,453 (95.3%) preferred immediate access. Respondents who reported abnormal results were also more likely to report increased worry. Both responses can be true for the same person. Someone may want to know quickly and still need help interpreting what they see. [Steitz and colleagues, JAMA Network Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2802672)

The survey reached people who had already used a portal to view a result. Fewer than one in five people who received the survey responded, and most respondents reported English as their primary language. Its percentages describe the respondents. They cannot tell us how a patient without a portal account would answer, or how someone would prefer to receive a specific result after discussing the options with their clinician. The study measured preferences and self-reported worry, with no randomized comparison of release policies.

A 2026 cross-sectional survey at Vanderbilt asked outpatients about worry while waiting for lab results and how their worry changed once they saw them. That distinction matters. A result might relieve uncertainty for one patient and raise new questions for another. The study relied on patients recalling their feelings and had no group randomly assigned to a different release policy. It helps describe the experience of seeing results in that setting. It leaves open how much worry any particular change to release timing would cause or prevent. [Steitz and colleagues, JAMA Network Open](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13684481/)

## Separate release from notification

A result can be available for a patient to look up without a push alert appearing on their phone. An alert changes when and how the patient first encounters the information. A notification while someone is travelling, at work or away from a private screen is different from opening the portal when they are ready. The design can preserve early access while giving patients some control over alerts, where the portal supports it.

Vanderbilt changed both release and notification policies in 2021. In an interrupted time-series study, immediate release with immediate alerts was associated with a fourfold increase in the proportion of results patients reviewed before a clinician and a reported 3% rise in the proportion of patients who initiated messages. When notifications became opt-in, both measures declined modestly. The researchers used changes over time at one centre. Other practices may have different patient populations, messaging norms and baseline release schedules. The study measured portal activity and messages, so it does not establish which messages were clinically necessary or whether the policy improved follow-up. [Steitz and colleagues, Journal of the American Medical Informatics Association](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10531100/)

Notification preferences and access also need local context. British Columbia’s Health Gateway provider guide, for example, says blood-test results are generally viewable two to three days after testing. It encourages clinicians to help patients plan for what they might see. A US study of immediate release can inform the design of a BC workflow, but its timing and rules should not be assumed to apply to Health Gateway or to every service in Canada. [BC Health Gateway provider guide](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/health/accessing-health-care/health-gateway/health-gateway-resources/health_gateway_-_clinic_guide_-_information_for_care_providers.pdf)

## Make the result easier to interpret

The laboratory report answers a narrow question about what was measured. The clinical interpretation combines the report with the reason for testing, the patient’s history and other findings. A number outside a reference interval can occur in someone who is healthy, and a number inside it does not rule out a medical problem. A comparison with an older value can also be misleading when tests use different methods, units or reference intervals. [MedlinePlus, understanding laboratory results](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/how-to-understand-your-lab-results/)

A portal can explain what an out-of-range flag means and make clear whether a comment comes from the laboratory or from the treating team. It should display units with the value and mark preliminary or incomplete reports where that distinction applies. Consider a hypothetical patient checking several tests from one blood draw. If two values post first, the screen should help them see that the workup is still in progress. A helpful message would tell them how the practice handles outstanding results and when it expects to communicate its interpretation. A colour or icon alone cannot convey those details.

Planning before the test gives patients a way to make sense of what happens next. The conversation can cover why the test was ordered, how results will arrive, who will discuss them and how to reach the team if the expected contact does not occur. For potentially sensitive findings, ask whether the patient wants someone with them for a discussion and how they can be reached privately. Offering a phone or other accessible route also matters for patients who share devices, have limited internet access or need language support. These are design choices to evaluate with the people who use the service. [Ontario Health, Patient Portal Provincial Implementation Guide](https://www.ontariohealth.ca/content/dam/ontariohealth/documents/patient-portal-provincial-implementation-guide.pdf)

## Give each result a clear route to follow-up

A systematic review found failures to follow up laboratory and imaging results across ambulatory settings. The underlying studies used different definitions of follow-up and were drawn from older workflows, making their rates a poor forecast for a particular clinic today. They establish why a practice needs to distinguish a result reaching an inbox from someone acting on it. A patient-side portal can add another opportunity to notice a missed result. The care team still needs a reliable process that works even when the patient never signs in. [Callen and colleagues, Journal of General Internal Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3445672/)

### Assign ownership before the result arrives

A practice can specify who reviews results, who covers absences and where results go if a clinician leaves or a patient changes services. Its process should address critical results through the local escalation pathway, independent of routine portal notifications. A signed review can record what the finding means and what action is necessary. When an action is required, the record can track which team member will contact the patient, the expected timeframe and how to confirm that any planned repeat test or referral took place. Responsibility across services is especially easy to lose when someone assumes a copied recipient has taken over.

A useful distinction is between *communicated* and *resolved*. Suppose a hypothetical result calls for a repeat test. A message describing the plan makes the recommendation available to the patient. Follow-up also depends on whether the repeat test is arranged, whether it happens and who reviews the new result. A team can decide how to track these steps, including when to escalate an unreachable patient or an overdue action. The original result can then be closed according to the clinic’s protocol. This design is an application of the failures described in the review. The review does not test this exact workflow. [Callen and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3445672/)

### Measure the outcome that matters

For a portal change, first define a population and a concrete safety question. A practice might examine outpatient lab results that require action and measure the proportion with a documented plan within a locally agreed interval. It should also measure time to patient contact and whether intended actions are completed. Record portal access and messaging alongside safety measures. A fall in messages could mean explanations improved, fewer people saw alerts or patients could not reach the clinic. An inbox count alone cannot distinguish those explanations.

Compare results with the previous workflow and inspect a sample of difficult cases, including people without active portal accounts and patients who prefer communication in another language. Check which tasks moved to nurses or administrative staff, and which generated extra work for patients. A larger study could compare different designs for notifications and explanatory text while keeping access and urgent escalation pathways in place. It should separately assess understanding, worry, time to action and missed follow-up. Our Journal guide to [turning a clinical observation into a research question](/blog/turning-a-clinical-observation-into-a-question/) describes how to narrow an observation like this into a measurable question.

## Conclusion

The available evidence shows strong support for prompt access among surveyed portal users and describes worry in some patients, particularly around abnormal findings. Studies of notification policies tell us more about viewing and messaging than about missed diagnoses or timely treatment. A portal can give patients early access and useful context. Clinical safety also depends on who reviews each result, how the team reaches the patient and whether the intended next step happens. Those processes should work for patients who use the portal and for those who never open it.

A team notices that some patients return shortly after a primary care visit. The observation is a starting point. On its own, it says little about whether a change in the service would help. A return could be a planned review, a new concern, or a sign that the original problem persisted.

## Define the outcome first

Suppose the question concerns unplanned return visits. Decide what counts as unplanned and over what period a return will be counted. Consider whether visits to another clinic can be observed. An apparently simple outcome often contains several decisions about measurement.

## Make the comparison explicit

A question about how frequently people return is descriptive. A question about whether a new follow-up process changes that frequency requires a comparison. For an intervention question, the PICO framework invites the reader to specify a population, intervention, comparator, and outcome.

In this example, the comparison might be a scheduled follow-up call versus the usual process after a first visit. The proposed call is hypothetical. Its value is a question for investigation.

## Check what the question leaves out

Fewer unplanned visits may sound desirable, but patients sometimes need timely reassessment. Asking how patients experienced follow-up, or whether they could obtain care when needed, may reveal a different aspect of the same service. Writing the question precisely helps readers see both its scope and its limits.

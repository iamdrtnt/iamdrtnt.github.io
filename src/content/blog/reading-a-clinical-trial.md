An abstract compresses a trial into a few hundred words. That makes it a useful entry point, but a difficult place to judge whether the findings answer a particular clinical question. Before reading the authors’ conclusion, try writing down the decision you hope the paper can inform.

## Reconstruct the comparison

Identify who could enter the trial, what participants received in each group, and how long follow-up lasted. The comparison group matters as much as the intervention. A trial comparing a new treatment with placebo answers a different question from one comparing the same treatment with the usual active alternative.

Look for the setting and recruitment criteria in the methods. A primary care service and a specialist referral clinic may see different patients even when the diagnosis is the same. This helps define who the result might apply to.

## Find the planned outcome

Locate the primary outcome and its assessment time. Check the protocol or registry record when it is available. If a paper emphasizes a different outcome in its abstract, explore the reason for that choice and keep the two analyses distinct in your notes.

An outcome can be measured precisely and still be distant from a patient’s immediate concern. Write down what changed in terms a reader could recognise, such as symptoms, a laboratory measure, or a hospital admission.

## Read the estimate in context

Consider the size of the difference and the uncertainty around it. For a binary outcome, counts and denominators can help you interpret a relative measure alongside the absolute difference between groups. Check how many participants contributed data to the analysis and what the researchers did about missing observations.

## Summarize what the trial measured

Finish with one sentence that names the participants, comparison, outcome, and follow-up period. If that sentence is hard to write, the missing element is a good reason to return to the full report.
